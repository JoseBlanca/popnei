# Performance review: the r² of every pair of variants, and the filter by linkage disequilibrium

23 September 2026. popnei gained two calculations on 23 September 2026 and
nobody has measured either of them. This review measures them for the
first time, says whether the matrix of r² reaches the 0.50 s that "Speed"
of `docs/specs/ld.md` asks of it, and settles the one number that document
left to a performance review. It was asked for by the owner when the plan
`docs/plans/ld.md` deferred its fourth work package, the one that measures,
into a review of its own.

The words this document uses. A **variant** is one position of a genome. A
**dosage** is, for one individual at one variant, how many of its alleles
are not the most frequent allele of that variant, so 0, 1 or 2 for a
diploid. **r²** is the squared correlation between the dosages of two
variants over the individuals both of them were called in; two variants
near each other on a chromosome usually have a high one, which is what
**linkage disequilibrium** means. A **block** is the run of variants a
reader gives at a time, here 5000 variants of 1000 individuals, held as one
array of one byte per allele. A **pass** is one reading of a whole dataset,
block after block, through a chain of filters. A **tile** is the square
block of variants the matrix of r² is worked out in, 256 variants when
this review began and 1000 now.
The **window** of the filter is the variants it has kept that are still
within `max_dist` base pairs of the variant it is looking at, on that
variant's chromosome. **Accelerate** is the linear algebra library of macOS
that popnei calls natively, the same one numpy calls, and **`dgemm`** is
its routine for the product of two matrices of `f64`. **faer** is the
linear algebra library written in Rust that popnei uses instead where
there is no Accelerate: in a browser always, and natively when the crate
is built with its `blas` feature off, which is how the same arithmetic is
run twice on one machine. **The cap** is the largest number of variants
`calc_r2_matrix` will take before it refuses, 5000 by default, which is
also the size every number in this report is of, since the matrix grows
with the square of the variants.

The two calculations. `calc_r2_matrix` gives the r² of every pair of the
variants of a pass, as one square matrix, 200 MB at the 5000 variants it
takes by default. It reads the pass into tiles and works the matrix out one
pair of tiles at a time; the r² of a pair of tiles comes from six matrix
products of three matrices per tile — the dosages, the called genotypes and
the squares of the dosages, each one row per variant and one column per
individual — and then one pass over the six results, cell by cell. Four
products are enough for a tile against itself. `Variants.filter_by_ld`
drops the variants that repeat what a variant kept near them already said;
per block it settles the variants in sets of 256, taking the r² of the
whole window against the whole set in one call and then the r² of the set
against itself.

## 1. The scope and its limits

The two calculations at commit `bce303c` of `main`: the module `ld` of the
core crate, the fourth filter in the module `filters`, `crates/popnei-linalg`
under both, and the two binding crates. The review works on the branch
`perf/ld`.

**Neither calculation has a benchmark or a profile in the repository at
that commit.** `crates/popnei/benches/` holds benches for the VCF reader,
the vars file, the three threshold filters, the Kosman distances and the
principal component analysis, and none for these two. So this review begins
by building the two benches that the deferred work package specifies. The
findings of section 5 were written before any of them ran, from reading
code and counting, so each is filed at the severity its evidence then
supported; section 8 says what each one turned out to be worth once there
were profiles and timings to decide it.

The machine is the owner's Apple M5 Pro, 18 cores — 6 performance cores
sharing 16 MB of second level cache and 12 efficiency cores — 64 GB, macOS,
native `aarch64-apple-darwin`, with Accelerate as the linear algebra
library. The dataset popnei measures itself on, from `docs/rust_core.md`,
is 100000 variants of 1000 individuals whose genotypes are missing at a
rate of 0.03: a 400 MB VCF and the 81 MB vars file popnei writes of it.

Nine categories were sent, one reviewer each: methodology, numbers,
allocations, data layout, concurrency, hot loops, the linear algebra and
wasm, the Python boundary, and input and output. None of them took a wall
time, so that they could read at once without measuring each other.

What was left out. The second item of `docs/specs/ld.md`, the curve of r²
against distance per population, which is specified and not built. The
browser, which is measured here for the first time, against no target,
since none was ever set for either calculation there, and with no profile:
the numbers of section 8 are wall times of the whole of each calculation
and say nothing about where the time goes inside it. And the sizes above
1000 individuals, where the shape of the products changes and nobody has
measured anything.

## 2. The verdict: apply, and two of the six candidates were wrong

**`calc_r2_matrix` reaches its target.** It took 0.454 s against the 0.50 s
of "Speed" of `docs/specs/ld.md` before anything was changed, and it takes
0.386 s now. Over 5000 variants of 1000 individuals read from the vars
file, the median of 5 runs with the machine idle, the 200 MB matrix
allocated inside what is timed and the reading of the file timed apart and
subtracted. The runs spread by 0.015 s.

Three changes were applied, each with its own commit and its own two
numbers, and each gated on the r² staying plink2's to the bit with the
tolerance of `assert_the_r2_is` set to exactly 0.0 by hand, on both
backends. Two of them bought time:

| | before | after | what it gained |
|---|---|---|---|
| the matrix, 5000 variants of 1000 individuals | 0.454 s | 0.386 s | 15% |
| the filter, 100000 variants, window of 249.2 | 1.814 s | 1.631 s | 10% |

The third bought memory and no time, which is what it was expected to buy:

| the matrix at the cap | before | after | |
|---|---|---|---|
| the peak of one call, from Python | 705527808 B | 502710272 B | 29% |
| what a tab keeps for its lifetime | 593 MB | 393 MB | 34% |

Of the six candidates this review was handed, three produced those changes,
one was measured and closed with no change, one is a decision for the owner
rather than a change, and **two were wrong about the code**: the method
they said copies the three matrices once per tile is called nowhere in the
library, and the loop they said writes 800 MB of scattered writes in fact
writes contiguously and strides its reads. Section 8 has each of them.

**The target prices about twice the arithmetic popnei is asked for.** It
comes from `docs/reports/ld-method/speed.py`, which takes six full
5000 x 5000 products in numpy: two of the six are the transposes of two
others, and it computes both halves of a symmetric matrix. popnei takes
only the pairs of tiles from the diagonal up, and four products instead of
six on the diagonal.

| | cells of the products | cells of the r² |
|---|---|---|
| numpy, `speed.py` | 150000000 | 25000000 |
| popnei, at the tile of 1000 it now uses | 76263680 | 13131840 |

So popnei does about half the arithmetic and takes 0.386 s against numpy's
0.455 s: per unit of arithmetic it is about 1.5 times slower than numpy on
Accelerate, through the same library on the same machine. Meeting 0.50 s
says the calculation is fast enough for a user; it does not say the code is
level with numpy. Whether the target should be restated is a decision for
the owner: it is section 9 here, and it was written into "Open points" of
`docs/specs/ld.md` as Open 3, which is where that spec keeps the questions
it leaves to the owner.

**Both calculations run on one core.** The matrix takes 0.388 s with
`VECLIB_MAXIMUM_THREADS` at 1 and 0.386 s with it unset; the filter takes
1.655 s on 18 threads and 2.147 s on 1 over a VCF, and the whole of that
difference is the parsing of the VCF, not the filter, which costs 1.55 s
either way. A product of 1000 variants by 1000 individuals by 1000 variants
is small enough that Accelerate does not split it across cores, and
popnei's own loop over the pairs of tiles is serial. 17 of the 18 cores of
this machine are idle for the whole of both calculations. Nothing was done
about it: the finding is L7 and what it would cost is there.

## 3. The measurement plan

In this order, because each unblocks what follows it. Every wall time is
taken with nothing else building, five runs at least, with the best, the
median and the worst printed, and each number is reported with its dataset,
its machine, its thread count and whether the allocation was inside or
outside what was timed. A difference under about 5% is confirmed by a
second clean measurement of each side, or by reverting and measuring again.

1. **The two benches**, which are deliverables 1 and 2 of the deferred work
   package. `crates/popnei/benches/r2_matrix.rs` times `calc_r2_matrix` on
   5000 variants of 1000 individuals read from a vars file, with the
   reading timed apart, and takes the tile size on its command line through
   the cargo feature `bench-internals` that `standardize_row` already uses.
   `crates/popnei/benches/filter_vars.rs` gains `--max-ld-r2` and
   `--max-dist`, so the fourth filter is priced by the same harness, the
   same warm-up and the same statistic as the three whose numbers are
   already in `docs/specs/filters.md`, and it prints what the window held.
2. **The baseline of both**, and the answer to whether the matrix reaches
   0.50 s at 5000 variants of 1000 individuals.
3. **A sampling profile of each**, `sample` on the bench process, to say
   how much of the time is inside Accelerate and how much is popnei's own.
4. **The experiments**, in the order of the findings below.
5. **The browser**, which is deliverable 3 of the work package.

Three things about how the filter must be measured, each of which would
have made its number wrong.

**Measure the filter over the vars file, not the VCF.** Its cost is the
difference between a pass with it and a pass without, and over the VCF that
difference is a tenth of each side: the published table for the three
threshold filters spans 0.036 s on a quantity of about 0.06 s, which needs
about 11 paired runs for ±0.010 s and about 44 for ±0.005 s. Over the vars
file the pass with no filter takes 0.102 s in every published row and the
difference is stated to ±1 ms with four pairs. The vars file is also where
the reader runs on the calling thread, so the threads are the filter's
alone. The VCF is measured too, as a second number with its own runs.

**The filter's baseline is not the same pass as the threshold filters'.**
`LdFilteredReader` adds the chromosome and the position to what the pass
asks for, which the three threshold filters do not. Over a VCF that turns
on position parsing and per-variant chromosome interning that a pass with
no filter never pays, so subtracting the two would charge the filter for
two parsed columns. Over the vars file those columns are read anyway.

**A number for this filter is meaningless without what the window held.**
How many variants a window holds is set by the dataset and by `max_dist`,
not by the filter. The 400 MB VCF has its variants 1000 base pairs apart on
two chromosomes of 50000 each, so `max_dist` sets the window almost exactly:
10000 base pairs is about 10 variants behind and 1000000 is about 1000. The
bench prints the mean and the largest number of kept variants within
`max_dist`, computed outside the clock, and every number is reported with
it. The filter is measured at more than one `max_dist` for that reason.

## 4. The build configuration

None of link-time optimization, one codegen unit, `target-cpu=native` or
`panic = "abort"` can change an r². rustc emits the multiply, the subtract
and the divide without the contract flag, so no pass fuses them whatever
the inlining; aarch64 has no excess precision, so an inlining decision
cannot change a rounding; and none of the four reaches Accelerate, which is
a prebuilt system library whose `dgemm` is the same code at every build
configuration. `docs/reports/perf-stats-2026-09-22.md` established the same
for another module on 22 September 2026 and found no fused multiply and add
at any optimization level.

The arithmetic of these two calculations is mostly inside that prebuilt
library, which link-time optimization cannot cross, so little is expected
from any of them. They are tried last, one at a time, and gated on a count
from `objdump -d` on the built benchmark rather than on a wall time, since
`cargo asm` forces its own codegen-units setting.

`[profile.bench]` and `[profile.profiling]` already carry the debug
information a sampling profile needs, and `.cargo/config.toml` sets no
native flags, so nothing there makes a timing unreproducible.

## 5. The findings

Numbered so that they can be referred to. None is above Likely, because
there is no profile.

### L1. The finiteness scan is run 122 times over every tile, and it may be most of the tile-size curve

`crates/popnei-linalg/src/lib.rs:307-308`. Likely, medium-high confidence,
found by the linear algebra reviewer; the numbers, allocations, hot loops
and concurrency reviewers each reported the same site independently.

Before every product, the linear algebra crate walks both operands to
refuse a value that is not finite. The six sums of a tile pair are six such
calls off the diagonal and four on it, so over a matrix of 20 tiles each
tile's three matrices are scanned 8 + 6 × 19 = 122 times: 610 million values,
4.88 GB. `docs/specs/pca.md` measured those scans on this same code at 1.3 ms
for 5500500 values, 0.236 ns each, which puts the scan at **0.144 s, or 29%
of the 0.50 s target**.

Nothing on this path needs it. `LdDosages` builds its three matrices from
whole numbers — the dosages are 0 to the ploidy, the called genotypes are 0
or 1, the squares are the dosages squared — and they are not changed after
they are built.

What makes this the first experiment is that it also explains the tile
curve. The scan costs one pass over the operands per call, which grows with
the tile in one dimension, where the product grows in two, so the scan's
share falls as one over the tile size. Fitting that shape to the measured
51.8, 38.0, 30.1 and 29.1 ns per pair of variants at tiles of 128, 256, 512
and 1024 leaves **29.7, 26.9, 24.6 and 26.3 ns, which is flat**. On that
reading `dgemm` barely cares about the tile size, and the curve the tile
decision was going to be made on is mostly an artefact of this check.

That gives a prediction which would refute the model if it failed: **once
the scan is hoisted, the best tile should reverse from large to small**,
because the only thing left that varies with the tile is the redundant
pairs a large diagonal tile computes, which grow with it — about 0.301 s at
128 and 0.309 s at 256 against 0.353 s at 1024.

Measurement plan: run the tile sweep before and after. The count is the
gate and is the same on every run: a static counter in the scan should fall
from 610 million values to 60 million, three matrices for each of 20 tiles.
Then the wall time, best of five, machine idle, spread reported.

What the measurement showed, in section 8: the scan is real and is 10.9% of
the calculation at a tile of 256, but it costs 0.048 s and not the 0.144 s
this fit predicts, so the fit is right about the shape and wrong about the
size by three times. The prediction that the best tile would reverse to
small once the scan was hoisted was never tested, because the scan was not
hoisted. Neither the fit nor the extrapolation of L2 predicted the curve
that was measured, which is why the tile was settled by a sweep.

Effect on the numbers: none. The same operands reach the same routine.

Complexity cost: a public type in `crates/popnei-linalg` whose name says
the values were checked already, built by a constructor that runs the scan
once, and the invariant that the buffer is not changed between the check
and the call. It changes "Errors" of `docs/specs/linalg.md`, and it reaches
`pca.rs`, which pays the same cost per block. A narrower version that only
hoists the scan within one `TheSumsOfThePairs::of` call — checking each
operand once instead of six times — needs no new type and no spec change,
and gets 610 million down to 122 million; that is the first thing to try.

### L2. The tile size: 256 today, and the evidence points at 512 before L1 and at 128 or 256 after it

`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:113`. Likely, medium confidence. The comment on
the constant says a performance review settles it.

Two reviewers recomputed the extrapolation from the measured per-pair
figures, independently, and got the same thing. The pairs of variants
popnei actually computes **rise** with the tile size, because a tile against
itself computes its whole square where the triangle needs half of it:

| tile | tiles | tile pairs | pairs of variants computed | products estimated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128 | 40 | 820 | 12819520 | 0.653 s |
| 256 | 20 | 210 | 13131840 | 0.483 s |
| 512 | 10 | 55 | 13756480 | 0.389 s |
| 1024 | 5 | 15 | 15005760 | 0.388 s |

The estimate charges an off-diagonal pair the measured rate and a diagonal
pair two thirds of it, since it takes four products and not six. The curve
bottoms at 512 and is flat to 1024: past 512 the falling rate per pair is
cancelled by the growing redundancy. The figure of "about 0.43 s at 256
against 0.35 s at 512" in `docs/reports/ld.md` does not follow from those
rates with any pair count either reviewer could reconstruct.

**At 256 the products alone would be 0.483 s of a 0.50 s target**, leaving
nothing for the dosage building, the element-wise pass, the writes and the
reader. That is what makes the tile worth settling and not merely tidying.

Measurement plan: the sweep at 128, 256, 512, 1024 and 1250 through the
bench, best of five, both with `VECLIB_MAXIMUM_THREADS` at 1 and unset,
with the tile pairs and the pairs computed printed beside each time so the
extrapolation is never needed again. Take the smallest tile whose whole
time is within 3% of the best. Run it again after L1.

Effect on the numbers: none, proved in section 2, and gated by the existing
test that compares the matrix to the bit at tiles of 7, 64, 256 and 500,
with the candidate size added to it.

Complexity cost: one constant. At 512 the 500-variant reference file stops
crossing a tile boundary, so the test must keep an explicit small tile.

Memory: the six sums of a tile pair are 3.1 MB at 256, 12.6 MB at 512 and
50.3 MB at 1024, beside a 200 MB matrix. In a browser, where a `usize` is
32 bits, that argues against the large end.

### L3. Both bindings copy the 200 MB matrix, and the core already offers to hand it over

`crates/popnei-python/src/ld.rs:221` and `crates/popnei-js/src/ld.rs:249`.
Likely, high confidence on the bytes, medium on the wall time. Found by the
Python boundary reviewer, with the allocations and data layout reviewers
concurring.

Each binding allocates a second copy of the matrix and writes it, so the
matrix is held twice — 400 MB at the cap — while it crosses. `R2Matrix::given_away`
exists in the core, is tested, and is called by no crate: `grep` for it
finds the definition, its doc comment and one test. Handing the vector over
means numpy takes the core's allocation with no copy, which the Kosman
distances in the same crate already do.

In a browser it is a ceiling rather than a leak. The memory is returned to
the allocator of the WebAssembly instance, so a second call reuses it, but
WebAssembly never returns pages to the host, so the peak of one call is
what the tab keeps for its lifetime. At two copies that peak caps a 4 GB
tab at about 16384 variants where one copy reaches the 23170 the TypeScript
package's documentation promises.

Measurement plan: the gate is deterministic and needs no wall time. For
Python, the maximum resident set size from `/usr/bin/time -l` should fall
by about 200 MB at the cap. For the browser, print the byte length of the
WebAssembly memory after the first call and after the second; the first
should grow about 200 MB less and the second should add nothing. The wall
time is the second check.

Effect on the numbers: none, the same bytes reach the caller.

Complexity cost: `given_away` consumes the matrix, so the chromosomes and
the positions are read before it and the reads are reordered. The error for
a machine that cannot give the memory of the copy becomes unreachable in
both bindings, and three doc comments and one line of the Python package
stop being true.

### L4. The per-variant dosage loop is the loop the principal component analysis review already fixed, with two further things blocking it

`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:1560`. Likely, high confidence, found by the hot
loops reviewer.

`the_dosages_of_a_variant` walks the genotypes of one variant with a chunk
length that is a run-time value, which is exactly the shape
`docs/reports/perf-pca-2026-09-22.md` found in `pca.rs` on 22 September
2026 and measured at **4.420 ms before and 0.325 ms after** for a block of
5000 × 1000. Here it has two blockers that `pca.rs` did not: a `continue`
for a genotype with an allele missing, and a carried `Option` that decides
whether the variant has two dosages. It also writes 24 bytes per genotype
where `pca` writes one.

It runs once per variant of a pass: 5 million genotypes for a matrix at the
cap, and for the filter 100 million for the dataset plus about 106 million
more from the window, which is rebuilt for every set.

Measurement plan: `cargo asm` on the function before and after, gated on a
deinterleaving vector load appearing and on the instructions per diploid
genotype, which in `pca` went from 34 per genotype to 15 per 16. Then the
two benches.

Effect on the numbers: none. The dosage and the missing flag are integers;
the square of a zero is the same zero the branch writes; and whether a
variant has two dosages becomes a comparison of the smallest and largest
called dosage, with no float and no dependence on order.

Complexity cost: the body twice, behind a match on the ploidy, as `pca.rs`
already has it, and a test that the two arms agree.

### L5. Half of every set the filter settles against itself is computed and never read

`crates/popnei/src/filters.rs:1221`. Likely, high confidence on the count,
medium on the gain. Found by the hot loops reviewer.

The filter takes the r² of a set of 256 candidates against itself, filling
all 65536 cells, and the loop that decides what stays reads only the strict
upper triangle. Over a pass of 100000 variants that is **12.76 million of
25.6 million cells computed for nothing**, and they are the cells whose
reads stride down a column, which is the slower half.

Effect on the numbers: none. The two cells of a pair are bit-identical
here, since the swap only exchanges the two operands of a multiplication.

Complexity cost: a second entry point with its own contract and tests. The
documented contract of `r2_between` must not change.

### L6. The window's three matrices are rebuilt for every set although only its ends changed

`crates/popnei/src/filters.rs:914`. Likely, medium confidence. Found by the
data layout reviewer, with hot loops and allocations concurring.

The window keeps the genotypes of its variants and expands them into the
three matrices for every set of 256 candidates. A variant's row never
changes once written. At 1000 individuals and a window of 270 kept
variants, over a pass of 100000 variants, that is **2.53 GB of `f64`
written**; at a window of 2500 it is 23.46 GB.

Effect on the numbers: none if each cached row is what `of_block` writes
for that variant, which it must be, since the row depends only on that
variant's genotypes.

Complexity cost: **this is a trade the spec already decided the other way,
so it is the owner's and not mine.** `docs/specs/filters.md` says the
window keeps genotypes and not dosages, at one byte per allele against 24
bytes per individual and variant, and caching the rows makes the window
twelve times larger: 5 MB to 60 MB at a window of 2500 variants of 1000
individuals. The matrices must also stay contiguous for the product, so
dropping variants off the front needs an offset and an occasional
compaction. It is in section 9.

### L7. Nobody knows whether Accelerate uses more than one thread at this shape, and the tile was chosen as if it did

`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:1356`. Likely, medium confidence. Found by the
concurrency reviewer.

`calc_r2_matrix` has no rayon, no thread, no lock and no atomic: it is one
serial loop over the tile pairs, and the only threads under it are
Accelerate's, inside the six products. A tile pair at 256 variants of 1000
individuals is a product of 131 MFLOP. If Accelerate's threshold for
splitting a product across cores is above that shape, all 1220 products of
a matrix run on one core and seventeen sit idle; if it is below, the matrix
pays a fork and a join 1220 times. The two cases call for opposite designs
and the code was written without knowing which holds.

Measurement plan: the bench at each tile size, twice, with
`VECLIB_MAXIMUM_THREADS` at 1 and unset, then a `sample` of the unset run
to count the Accelerate threads and the share of their samples that is a
wait. If the two settings differ by less than 5% at 256 while differing at
1024, Accelerate is serial at tile size and threading the tile-pair loop is
the only way to use the machine.

Effect on the numbers: none, and there is already a test that the matrix
does not change with the number of threads.

### L8. The element-wise pass is serial and is the only part popnei can thread without nesting two pools

`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:567`. Likely, medium confidence. Found by the
concurrency reviewer.

Each cell of the result is a function of its own six inputs, so the rows
can be split with no reduction and the result is bit-identical. The pass
reads six values and writes one per cell, about 13 operations, which is
memory-bound: threads buy the ratio of aggregate to single-core bandwidth,
not the core count, and part of the working set was just written by the
products and is still in cache.

Threading the tile-pair loop instead is the larger prize and the larger
cost: 210 pairs each calling Accelerate is the nesting that decision 6 of
`docs/rust_core.md` forbids, Accelerate cannot be pinned for one call, and
the core crate may use no `unsafe`, so writing disjoint parts of one matrix
from several threads needs either bands plus a mirroring pass over 200 MB
or per-pair results collected and written serially. It is filed and not
recommended until L7 says whether the cores are idle.

### L9. The mirror half of a tile pair strides its reads, not its writes

`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:1443`. Likely, medium confidence. Found
independently by the data layout and hot loops reviewers, **and it corrects
the candidate list this review started from.**

`docs/reports/ld.md` describes this loop as about 800 MB of scattered
writes. It is the other way round. The writes are a run of adjacent cells,
2048 bytes at a tile of 256, aligned to the 128-byte cache line of this
machine: full-line stores. What strides is the read of the tile buffer,
2048 bytes between one value and the next, over a 512 KB buffer that does
not fit the 128 KB first-level cache. At the cap that is about 12.45
million strided reads against the 4096 line fetches per pair a blocked pass
would take. They hit in the 16 MB second-level cache rather than main
memory, so it is a latency cost and not a bandwidth one, and the gain is
expected to be small.

The way to measure it apart from the products, which a reviewer of the plan
asked for: build the bench under the profile that keeps full debug
information and read the share of samples in this function; then an A/B
against a build with the loop deleted, which gives a wrong matrix and is
for timing only.

Complexity cost: one more level of loop nesting and a block constant, in a
function that reads plainly today.

### Speculative

**S1. A fast path for a tile pair with no missing genotype turns six
products into one.** `crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:740`. `docs/specs/ld.md`
names this in "How it runs" and leaves it to the plan to decide on a
measurement; the plan never decided it. If every genotype of both tiles is
called, the count of individuals is the same for every pair and the four
remaining sums are row sums, one value per variant rather than one per
pair, so only the product of the dosages with themselves is a real product.
It cannot help the dataset popnei measures itself on, whose genotypes are
missing at 3 in 100, so no tile of 256 variants will be complete; it helps
imputed or complete datasets and anything behind a strict missing-data
filter. The bench must therefore carry a fully-called dataset too. It is
the largest complexity cost of anything here: a second path through the
hottest function, a flag on the dosages and a branch in the sums.

**S2. `dsyrk` for the two symmetric products of a diagonal tile pair.**
`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:762`. Two of the four products of a tile against
itself are symmetric and the linear algebra crate already has a routine
that does half the work of a general product. The ceiling is about 1.6% of
the matrix at a tile of 256 and about 6% at 1024, so it is worth pairing
with L2 and not worth running alone. It writes only one triangle, so either
the triangle is mirrored, which eats part of the gain, or the element-wise
pass learns to read a triangle.

**S3. The six products in `f32`.** Every partial sum is a whole number, and
an `f32` counts integers one by one to 16777216, which at the objectives'
largest dataset of 10000 diploid individuals is 419 times the largest sum.
So the products would be bit-identical below a stated bound, halve the
bytes they stream, and reach a routine with twice the peak rate. The cost
is a second element type through the linear algebra crate, two backends,
a new bound and a fallback. Filed, not run.

**S4. `n` as a count of set bits.** The matrix of called genotypes is 0 or
1, so the count of individuals a pair shares is an intersection: 16 words
per variant at 1000 individuals against 1000 multiply-and-adds. It removes
one of six products at the cost of a fourth representation of the
individuals. Filed, not run.

### Notes

**N1. `LdDosages::rows` is called nowhere in the library.** `crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:403`.
Every use of it is in the test module. It is public API that an outside
caller could use, and its own doc comment, which says the tiles and the
window take it, is wrong. **This corrects the candidate list this review
started from**: it costs nothing per pass and there is no experiment.

**N2. The matrix is filled with NaN and then every cell is written again.**
`crates/popnei/src/ld.rs:1340`. 200 MB of writes and first touch per call
that the tile loop then overwrites. Same shape as the buffers of L1's
sibling finding: `a_vector_of` reserves and then fills a value that the
product overwrites, because `dgemm` is called with a beta of zero. Over a
matrix that is 610 MB asked of the allocator and zeroed for nothing, in
1220 allocations; over a filter pass, 6.94 GB in 8800 allocations. The fix
is a scratch buffer handed in, as `pca.rs` already has for its rows. It is
a Note and not a finding of its own only because its gain is unknown until
the profile says how much of the time is in the allocator.

**N3. The window's `drain` moves its tail once per set**, 0.211 GB over a
pass at a window of 270 and 1.955 GB at 2500 — twelve times less than L6 on
the same window, so it is not where to start.

**N4. The filter's cost cannot be bounded by the bench's thread count.**
The bench runs a pass inside a rayon pool, so the filter's products are
called from a worker of that pool, which is the nesting decision 6 forbids
and which `docs/specs/ld.md` says does not happen. Nothing of the fourth
filter runs on that pool: it never uses the parallel row scan the three
threshold filters use. So the "18 threads" column of `docs/specs/filters.md`
measures the parser for this filter and the filter's own row scan for the
other three, and the two columns are not comparable. Whatever the numbers
turn out to be, that sentence goes into "Speed".

## 6. Seen outside the scope

- `crates/popnei-python/src/ld.rs:93` says the products of the tiles are
  the work of every core of the machine. The code has no rayon and one
  serial loop, and whether Accelerate uses more than one core at this shape
  is L7.
- `crates/popnei-linalg/Cargo.toml` says the flag that emits the vector
  instructions of WebAssembly is not set; `.cargo/config.toml` sets it for
  both wasm targets.
- `js/popnei/src/ld.ts` promises 23170 variants in a 4 GB tab; with the
  copy of L3 the real ceiling is about 16384.
- `docs/specs/ld.md` gives 1.9 ms for a tile pair of 256, which is 29.0 ns
  a variant pair, where `docs/reports/ld.md` gives 38.0 ns for the same
  quantity, and the same report's 7.40 ms at 512 is 28.2 ns against the
  spec's 5.7 ms, which is 21.7 ns. Two live figures for one thing, 23 to
  31% apart. This review replaces both with measurements of popnei.
- **One pytest test passes or fails by the build profile.**
  `tests/test_io_vars.py::test_a_ctrl_c_while_write_vars_runs_is_raised_and_leaves_no_file`
  sends itself an interrupt 0.1 s after `write_vars` starts and expects to
  catch it. Built with `uv run maturin develop`, which is what the `coding`
  skill runs, the write is still going and the test passes: 347 passed.
  Built with `uv run maturin develop --release`, which this review used
  because it was about to take timings, the write finishes first and the
  test fails with "DID NOT RAISE KeyboardInterrupt". Nothing of this review
  caused it and nothing here depends on it, but a test whose result is set
  by how the extension was built is one that will fail in whatever builds
  release, and the seconds it waits are a constant in that file.
- **A pass over the 400 MB dataset driven from Python, block by block, took
  3.068 s where the same pass inside Rust takes 0.106 s.** That figure was
  taken while the browser numbers were being measured, to see whether the
  filter could be timed through the Python binding, and was discarded for
  that purpose because it swamps what it was meant to measure. The two are
  not the same work — the Python side builds arrays for each block — so the
  29 times is not a defect until somebody measures it properly. Nothing in
  this review depends on it, and no other calculation of popnei is timed
  that way: the principal component analysis, the Kosman distances and this
  matrix all cross into Rust once per call. It is worth a measurement of
  its own because a user who iterates blocks in Python pays it.
- The curve of r² against distance, which is specified and not built, sums
  r² into bins. Those sums are not whole numbers, so unlike everything in
  this review its result would depend on the tile size and the order of the
  tiles. Whoever builds it needs to know that.

## 7. What the code already does well

- **The layout of the three matrices is the right one for Accelerate.**
  One row per variant and one column per individual, as three separate
  vectors, makes every one of the six products the form in which both
  operands are read along contiguous rows, which is why the second operand
  carries its layout in its type and nothing is transposed or copied.
  Interleaving the three would break all six.
- **The diagonal tile really does take four products and not six**, on a
  pointer comparison, and the two sums it skips are read off the other two
  transposed, with the argument for why that is exact to the bit written
  out where it is done.
- **The buffer for the r² of one tile pair is allocated once for the
  largest tile and sliced per pair**, and the genotype buffer that the
  tiles are poured into is reused across the whole pass. The per-block
  buffers of the filter are hoisted the same way.

## 8. What the experiments showed

Every timing below is of the owner's Apple M5 Pro, 18 cores, macOS, native,
with Accelerate, over the 400 MB dataset of `docs/rust_core.md` read from
the vars file popnei writes of it, already in the page cache, with nothing
else running. A number is the median of 5 runs with the best and the worst
beside it. After every change the r² was checked against plink2 **to the
bit**, by setting the tolerance of `assert_the_r2_is` to exactly 0.0 by
hand and running the tests on Accelerate and on faer, and the filter was
checked to keep 84, 133, 85 and 85 variants of the reference dataset.
`tests/reference/ld/run_plink2.sh` was run once into an empty directory and
exited 0 naming no differing file; it runs plink2 and a Python rule and no
Rust, so no change of this review could move it.

### The baseline, which nobody had

At commit `f6abd5e`, with the two benches in place: the matrix of 5000
variants of 1000 individuals took **0.454 s**, of which 0.005 s was reading
the file, against a target of 0.50 s. A pass of the filter over 100000
variants at a threshold of 0.3 and a `max_dist` of 250000, where the window
held 249.2 variants, took **1.814 s** against 0.107 s for the same pass
with no filter.

### L2, the tile: applied at 38d73e9

The sweep over the matrix of 5000 variants, less the reading:

| tile | 128 | 256 | 512 | 1000 | 1024 | 1250 | 2500 | 5000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | 0.539 s | 0.449 s | 0.424 s | 0.382 s | 0.414 s | 0.380 s | 0.403 s | 0.427 s |

Neither extrapolation this review was handed predicted that curve: both put
the bottom at 512, and both were built by charging a fixed cost to each
pair of variants. The bottom is a flat basin from 1000 to 1250, and 1024 is
8% slower than 1000 for a tile 2% larger, because 5000 does not divide by
1024 and the short last tile is a badly shaped product. The tile is 1000
rather than 1250 because the basin moves with the size of the matrix and
1000 is the better of the two away from the cap: 0.255 s against 0.263 s
over 4000 variants, 0.147 s against 0.150 s over 3000.

The matrix is the same to the bit at every tile, which the benchmark's own
checksum shows — 224076.635484 over 0 values that are not a number, at
every size in the table — and which
`neither_the_blocks_nor_the_tiles_change_the_matrix` now asserts at the
default tile as well as at 7, 64, 256 and 500.

### L4, the per-variant dosage loop: applied at 0c75b20

`the_dosages_of_a_variant` walked the genotypes of a variant with a chunk
length the compiler did not know, so the loop over the individuals stayed
scalar behind tests for 64 and for 8 alleles that a ploidy of 2 never
takes. Written with the ploidy a compile-time constant, as
`docs/reports/perf-pca-2026-09-22.md` had already done for the same shape
in `pca.rs`, the machine code gains a deinterleaving vector load and goes
from **46 instructions per called diploid genotype to 7.25**, a factor of
6.3.

| | baseline | after | reverted | re-applied |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the filter, `max_dist` 250000 | 1.820 s | 1.628 s | 1.805 s | 1.623 s |
| the filter, `max_dist` 1 | 0.787 s | 0.691 s | 0.790 s | 0.700 s |
| the matrix, less the reading | 0.383 s | 0.376 s | 0.379 s | 0.377 s |

The matrix gain is under 5%, so it was confirmed by reverting, rebuilding,
measuring and re-applying, which is the four columns above. The filter
gains 10%. The matrix gains about 1%, which is the size the profile
predicted, since the three functions were 1.5% of it, and is at the edge of
what these runs resolve, so it should not be quoted to two figures.

What it cost: the loop body exists twice, once with the ploidy a constant
for the ploidies 1 to 4 and once reading it from the dataset, behind a
match, which is what `pca.rs` already does. The carried `Option` that
decided whether a variant has two dosages became a lowest and a highest
dosage, which makes **a new invariant somebody has to keep**. The loop
now keeps the lowest and the highest dosage it has seen among the genotypes
that were called, and answers "this variant has two dosages at least" with
`highest > lowest`. A genotype that was not called offers 255 to the lowest
and 0 to the highest, so a variant called in nobody ends with a lowest of
255 and a highest of 0. Those two never get swapped back, so the test has
to be `>` and not `>=`: with `>=` a variant called in nobody would compare
0 against 255, fail the test, and be reported as having two dosages, where
it has none. One test came with it, comparing the two loop bodies at each
of the ploidies 1 to 4.

### L1, the finiteness scan: measured, no change, closed

Before every product the linear algebra crate walks both operands to refuse
a value that is not finite. A sampling profile of 7107 samples put it at
10.9% of the matrix at a tile of 256, the largest thing popnei does outside
Accelerate. Taking both scans out in a build made only for the measurement,
and putting them back:

| | the scan as it is | the scan taken out | what it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| tile 256 | 0.449 s | 0.401 s | 0.048 s, 10.7% |
| tile 1000 | 0.383 s | 0.366 s | 0.017 s, 4.4% |

**The tile change took two thirds of it for free**, because the scan runs
once per call and a larger tile makes fewer calls. What is left is 4.4% of
the calculation, and hoisting it needs a type in `crates/popnei-linalg`
that carries the promise that the caller has already checked, which changes
"Errors" of `docs/specs/linalg.md` and reaches `pca.rs` too. That is the
same trade `docs/reports/perf-pca-2026-09-22.md` refused on 22 September
2026, on the same scans, at 5% there. **Closed: the gain does not justify
the spec change.** It is worth revisiting only for both modules together,
if the linear algebra crate ever changes that interface for another reason.

### L7, the threads: measured, no change, for the owner

| | one thread | as they come |
|---|---|---|
| the matrix, 5000 variants | 0.388 s | 0.386 s |
| the filter over the vars file, `max_dist` 250000 | 1.631 s | 1.631 s |
| the filter over the VCF, `max_dist` 250000 | 2.147 s | 1.655 s |

The first two rows are the same number: Accelerate does not split a product
of 1000 by 1000 by 1000 across cores, and popnei's own loops are serial.
The third row moves only because the VCF is parsed on the pool — a pass
with no filter over the same file goes from 0.600 s to 0.086 s — so the
filter costs about 1.55 s either way. One set of 5 runs at 18 threads over
the VCF gave a worst of 2.388 s against a best of 1.629 s; three other sets
were stable to 0.03 s, so that one is an outlier and not a pattern, and the
spread is recorded because a single figure would hide it.

Nothing was changed. Threading the loop over the pairs of tiles is the
prize and is what decision 6 of `docs/rust_core.md` forbids; it is in
section 9.

### What the filter costs, which nothing had measured

Over the vars file on one thread, against a pass with no filter of 0.106 s,
after both changes:

| threshold | max_dist | the window held | kept of 100000 | the pass | the filter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 1 | 0.0 variants | 100000 | 0.696 s | 0.590 s |
| 0.3 | 10000 | 10.0 | 99994 | 0.840 s | 0.734 s |
| 0.3 | 50000 | 50.0 | 99974 | 0.991 s | 0.885 s |
| 0.3 | 250000 | 249.2 | 99919 | 1.631 s | 1.525 s |
| 0.3 | 1000000 | 988.5 | 99850 | 4.184 s | 4.078 s |
| 0.02 | 250000 | 88.7 | 35673 | 1.107 s | 1.001 s |
| 0.005 | 250000 | 13.7 | 5759 | 0.849 s | 0.743 s |

Two things this says that no single number would. **0.590 s is paid before
the window holds anything**, which the first row isolates by making
`max_dist` 1 so that no variant is ever in another's window: that is the
three matrices built for every variant of the pass and the r² of every set
of candidates against itself. And what moves the rest is the window and not
how many variants survive: at one `max_dist` and three thresholds the pass
goes from 1.631 s to 0.849 s as the window falls from 249.2 variants to
13.7, while the variants kept fall from 99919 to 5759.

This dataset's variants carry no linkage, so almost nothing is pruned at a
threshold of 0.3 and every window stays full. That is this filter's worst
case, and a dataset with linkage costs less, which the last two rows show
from the other side.

### The browser, which nobody had measured either

On the wasm package built with `npm run build` in `js/popnei` and run under
node v26.8.2, on one thread, with the 128-bit vector instructions of
WebAssembly, where the linear algebra is faer and not Accelerate. The
median of 5 runs, the file opened outside every timing.

| | a browser | natively | how many times |
|---|---|---|---|
| the matrix of 5000 variants, less the reading | 5.616 s | 0.380 s | 14.8 |
| reading 5000 variants | 0.006 s | 0.006 s | 1.0 |
| a pass with no filter, 100000 variants | 0.114 s | 0.106 s | 1.1 |
| the filter, `max_dist` 250000, window 249.2 | 18.402 s | 1.631 s | 11.3 |
| the filter, `max_dist` 1, window 0 | 7.483 s | 0.696 s | 10.8 |

**There is no target for either calculation in a browser**, so none of this
is a pass or a miss; it is the first measurement of one. Reading costs a
browser almost nothing more, so the whole of the difference is arithmetic.
The share the filter pays before its window holds anything is two fifths in
both builds.

The two builds compute the same thing: every browser run printed the
25000000 values of the matrix adding to 224076.635484 with none of them not
a number, which is the native figure to all six decimals, and the filter
kept 99919 of 100000 in both.

The whole 100000-variant file runs in a browser and nothing had to be cut
down: the filter's passes held 229 MB of WebAssembly memory against the few
GB a tab gives.

### What the browser says about the copy each binding makes

The matrix at the cap holds **593 MB of WebAssembly memory**, reached on the
first call and never added to afterwards. What accounts for it: the matrix
200 MB, the copy the binding makes of it on the way to JavaScript another
200 MB, the three matrices of the five tiles 120 MB, the six sums of one
pair of tiles 48 MB and the file 6 MB, which is 574 MB of the 593 MB.

That was the first measured evidence for L3, and it is what decided to run
it. WebAssembly gives no page back to the host, so the high-water mark of
one call is what the tab keeps for its lifetime.

### L3, the copy each binding made: applied at 4abfd85

Both bindings now take the matrix from the core with `R2Matrix::given_away`
instead of copying it. The core crate is untouched: the change is 59 lines
added and 151 removed across the two binding crates, the Python package and
two doc comments.

| the matrix at the cap | before | after | |
|---|---|---|---|
| the peak of one call, from Python | 705527808 B | 502710272 B | 202817536 B, 29% |
| the WebAssembly memory a tab keeps | 592904192 B | 392888320 B | 200015872 B, 34% |
| the call, from Python, median of 5 | 0.384 s | 0.380 s | |
| the call, in a browser, median of 5 | 5.783 s | 5.614 s | |

**The memory is the gate and the wall time is not the point.** Neither time
moved: the browser's baseline had a worst run of 6.187 s against 5.623 s
after, so the 3% is the spread of the runs. The checksum is 224076.635484
over 0 values that are not a number on both sides, before and after, in
every run of both languages.

**A trap in how this has to be measured, which cost the first pair of runs.**
The maximum resident set size of a process is a high-water mark that never
falls, and `numpy.nansum`, which the checksum uses, copies the whole array
it is given — another 200 MB at the cap. So a script that checksums the
matrix and then reads the peak reports the peak of the checksum and hides
the whole effect: measured that way the figure was 735035392 B before and
728678400 B after, which says nothing moved. The number that decides is the
peak taken after the call and before anything reads the matrix.

What it cost: a Python user no longer gets a separate error when the
machine can hold the matrix once but not twice, because there is no second
allocation left to fail, and that error variant is gone from the Python
binding. **A machine that cannot hold the matrix at all is still refused**,
by the core, with the same `ValueError` as before. The threshold moved from
"fits once but not twice" to "fits once", which lets a machine do what it
could not do before. The TypeScript package promises 23170 variants in a
4 GB tab; with the copy the true ceiling was about 15550 and without it is
about 21550, so that promise is closer to honest than it was and was left
as it stands.

### What was not run, and why

- **L5, half of every set the filter settles against itself.** 12.76
  million of 25.6 million cells per pass are computed and never read. Not
  run: it needs a second entry point with its own contract and tests, and
  the profile puts the whole element-wise pass at 6% of the filter, so the
  most it can give is about 3%.
- **L6, caching the window's dosages.** Not run because it is the owner's:
  it reverses a trade `docs/specs/filters.md` made deliberately. Section 9.
- **L8, L9, S1 to S4 and the build configuration.** Not run. L9 turned out
  to be 3.8% of the matrix, and the reviewers found its mechanism was the
  opposite of what the candidate list said, so its ceiling is small. The
  build configuration was left last and not reached; section 4 says why
  little is expected of it.

### Two of the six candidates were wrong about the code

Both were found by reviewers reading the code, and both are recorded so the
next review does not chase them.

**`LdDosages::rows` is called nowhere in the library.** The candidate list
has it copying the three matrices once per tile. Every use of it is in the
test module: the tiles are built by pouring the blocks into one shared
buffer and calling `of_block`, and the filter's window uses
`the_dosages_of`. It is public API that an outside caller could use, and
its own doc comment, which says the tiles and the window take it, is wrong.

**The transposed half of a tile pair strides its reads, not its writes.**
The candidate list has it as about 800 MB of scattered writes. The writes
are runs of adjacent cells aligned to this machine's 128-byte cache line;
it is the read of the tile buffer that strides. That changes what a fix
would be, and makes the candidate smaller than it looked: the profile puts
the whole of writing the tile pairs into the matrix at 3.8%.

## 9. What is asked of the owner

**Whether the 0.50 s target should stand.** It was set from numpy taking
0.455 s for six full 5000 x 5000 products, plus a tenth. popnei does about
half that arithmetic, 76263680 cells of products against 150000000, because
it takes only the pairs of tiles from the diagonal up and four products
instead of six on the diagonal. It now takes 0.386 s, so it meets the
target. Keeping 0.50 s means the spec states what a user waits for, and the
calculation meets it. Restating it as about 0.25 s, the like-for-like
figure, means the spec states whether the code is level with numpy, against
which popnei is 1.5 times slower through the same library on the same
machine — and then the spec states a target popnei does not meet and no
experiment here found a way to halve the remaining time, since 72.7% of it
is inside Accelerate. Recommendation: keep 0.50 s and record the
like-for-like figure beside it, which "Speed" of `docs/specs/ld.md` now
does, as Open 3 of that spec. Nothing is blocked on this.

**Whether the filter's window should hold dosages instead of genotypes.**
"How it runs" of the filter item of `docs/specs/filters.md` decided that
the window keeps the genotypes of its variants, one byte per allele, and
builds the three matrices of them again for every set of candidates it
settles, because the matrices are 24 bytes for each individual and each
variant, twelve times more. Over a pass of 100000 variants of 1000
individuals at a window of 270 kept variants, that rebuilding writes 2.53
GB of `f64`; at a window of 2500 it writes 23.46 GB. Keeping the rows
instead would make the window 60 MB rather than 5 MB at a window of 2500,
and the matrices must stay contiguous for the product, so dropping variants
off the front needs an offset and an occasional compaction, which is an
invariant somebody has to keep. The gain in seconds was not measured,
because the change is the reversal of a decision the spec already made and
that is yours; a profile of the filter at a window of 249.2 variants puts
the whole of building the dosages, the window's and the candidates'
together, at 17% of the pass before the change of section 8 and a little
under half of that after it. Recommendation: leave it, and revisit only if
a user reports a pass that is too slow on a dataset with little linkage,
which is where the window grows.

**Whether popnei may run its own threads around a call into Accelerate.**
Decision 6 of `docs/rust_core.md` says rayon owns the per-variant work,
BLAS has its own pool for the big products, and the two must not nest. This
review measured what that costs here: Accelerate does not split a product
of 1000 variants by 1000 individuals by 1000 variants across cores, and
popnei's loop over the pairs of tiles is serial, so **both calculations run
on one of the 18 cores of this machine**. The matrix is 0.386 s and a pass
of the filter 1.631 s, both on one core, with 17 idle. Threading the loop
over the tile pairs is the only way to use them, and it is the nesting
decision 6 forbids: Accelerate cannot be pinned for one call, only for the
whole process, so a library that threads that loop cannot hold up its side
of the bargain on a user's machine. It also needs either bands of tile rows
plus a second pass over the 200 MB matrix, or per-tile-pair results
collected and written serially, because the core crate may use no `unsafe`.
What it is worth is not known: nobody has measured what Accelerate does at
this shape with several callers at once. Recommendation: leave decision 6
as it is and file the question, unless a user needs the matrix of 5000
variants in well under 0.386 s, which no one has asked for.

**Two decisions this review does not ask you to make here.** They were
opened by `docs/reports/ld.md` on 23 September 2026 and nothing measured
here bears on either, so their options, their costs and their
recommendations are in that report and are not repeated: whether
`has_variance` and `maf` of `LdDosages` should stop answering the same for
a variant that is there and has no data and for a number that is not a
variant of the set at all; and whether popnei's convention of exceptions
should gain a fourth case for a machine that has not the memory. They are
named here only so that this report's list of what is open is the whole
list. Neither blocks anything in section 8, and the changes applied here
stand whichever way you settle them.
