# Performance review: the VCF reader and the vars file

22 September 2026. The review of `io::vcf`, which reads a VCF and gives
its variants in blocks, and of `io::vars`, which writes and reads the vars
file, popnei's own file of variants. Both are at commit `d1c03f8`, the tip
of `main`, and neither was changed by this review: it says where the time
goes and which experiments are worth running, and the experiments have not
been run yet. The branch is `perf/io`.

The words this document uses, in the order it needs them. The **reader**
of a VCF gives **blocks**, runs of consecutive variants held as arrays;
for the 403 MB file below a block is 2500 variants of 1000 diploid
individuals. Inside a block the reader works in **batches** of lines, 4096
at most: it reads the lines of a batch one after another on the thread
that called it, the **reading thread**, and then parses them on the
threads of rayon, the **workers**, each line into its own row. The pass
that reads the lines is the **serial line pass**. A vars file is one arrow
file whose **record batches** are the blocks; reading one means
decompressing it with lz4. **Self time** is the share of a profile's
samples in which a function was at the top of the stack, the work it did
itself rather than in what it called. A **gate** is what decides an
experiment: a count where the mechanism can be counted, because a count is
the same on every run, and a wall time otherwise.

## 1. The verdict, and what is asked of the owner

**Every target of both specs is met at this commit**, measured again for
this review on a quiet machine: the four VCF reads at 0.570, 0.087, 0.827
and 0.372 s against targets of 0.594, 0.108, 0.924 and 0.44 s, and a pass
over a vars file at 20.47 ms against 21 ms. Section 3.6 has the table.

**Run the experiments.** There are candidates with a matching profile and
a contained cost, and section 6 orders them. Nothing here is ready to
apply without its measurement. Two of the candidates were found by two
reviewers of different categories working apart, which is why they lead.

Four things are the owner's to decide, and the rest of the review does
not depend on them:

- **Whether the VCF reader is held to the spike or to plink2.** The spec
  holds it to the spike, the trial parser in Rust, and by that number all
  four targets are met. plink2 reads the same file in 0.273 s on one
  thread against popnei's 0.563 s, and plink2 is one of the outside
  references goal 4 of `docs/objectives.md` names. Section 3.5 says what
  the two numbers do and do not have in common.
- **Whether the vars writer gets a speed target.** It has none. It has
  been measured once, 39.0 to 39.3 ms for four blocks into memory, and a
  target taken from popnei's own single measurement would only freeze
  today's behaviour. Section 4.4 says what would give a target with an
  outside program on its other side.
- **Whether a caller may ask for an uncompressed vars file.** Reading one
  took arrow-rs 1 ms against lz4's 19 on the panel of "The compression"
  of `docs/specs/io_vars.md`. Nothing in the spec says a caller may ask
  for one. In a browser tab, where the bytes are already in memory and
  there is no disc to save, the trade is a different one than on a laptop.
- **Whether writing a vars file from Python should still wait for the
  disc to confirm the bytes.** That wait is 7 to 11 in 100 of the call.
  Finding N2 in section 6.4 has what it buys and what dropping it would
  and would not protect against; it is a change of a promise the
  docstring makes, not an experiment.

## 2. The limits of this review

**The baselines below were taken on a quiet machine; the profiles were
not.** The machine was shared with another session for most of this
review, at a load average of 3.0 to 7.4, and every sampling profile in
section 3 was taken then. A profile's *ranking* survives that, because
every frame is slowed alike; a wall time does not, and the same read that
comes out at 0.570 s in section 3.6 came out at 0.594 to 0.647 s under the
load. The machine went quiet at the end and the seven numbers of section
3.6 were taken then, at a load average of 0.93 to 1.00, with nothing else
building. Any experiment of section 4 has to be run the same way, and two
experiments must never be timed at once.

What else was left out:

- **The VCF reader has never been run in wasm, in either target.** The
  vars reader has: `docs/reports/perf-dists-kosman-2026-09-22.md` timed a
  pass over `big.vars`, 100000 variants of 1000 individuals, at 0.113 s
  under node against 0.107 s natively on one thread, after `+simd128`
  vectorized the decompression, and 5 to 10 ms of the wasm figure is a
  copy into a typed array that the native path does not make. So the vars
  read costs a tab about a tenth more than a laptop, and what has no
  number at all is the VCF read. Section 4.5 says what the first such
  measurement would be.
- **No allocation count was taken.** DHAT is the `dhat` crate behind a
  cargo feature and the feature does not exist yet; section 4.2 has the
  shape it would take, which is itself one of the review's deliverables.
- **The bgzipped read was not profiled.** It is 0.890 s on one thread
  against the plain file's 0.563 s, and where that difference goes has
  never been read off a profiler.
- **Neither module has been run at the sizes the objectives name**, a
  million variants or 10000 individuals. `wide10000.vcf`, 3000 variants of
  10000 individuals, exists in the benchmark directory and has never been
  read by a benchmark.

## 3. Where the time goes

### 3.1 The VCF read on one thread: one function holds nine tenths of it

`/usr/bin/sample`, 25 s, on `big.vcf`, 100000 variants of 1000 diploid
individuals, 403572954 bytes, the genotypes alone asked for, one thread,
the bench profile. 21139 samples of the thread that reads, taken at
`d1c03f8` for this review:

```
Sort by top of stack, same collapsed (when >= 5):
        __psynch_cvwait                                21139
        popnei::io::vcf::fill_row_genotypes            19289
        core::slice::memchr::memchr_aligned              717
        read                                             442
        core::str::converts::from_utf8                   235
        _platform_memmove                                167
        core::str::iter::Split<char>::next                98
        _platform_memset                                  61
        popnei::io::vcf::BatchRow::parse                  31
        _platform_memcmp                                  28
        popnei::io::vcf::next_column                      18
```

The 21139 of `__psynch_cvwait` are the main thread asleep on the rayon
latch while the work runs; they are not work. `fill_row_genotypes` is
**19289 of 21139, 91.2 in 100** of the reading thread. `gt_of`,
`fill_row_genotype` and `parse_row_allele` are inlined into it and have no
frame of their own, so the profile cannot say which of them holds the
share. That function runs once per individual per variant, 1e8 times for
this file.

The profile of 21 September that `docs/specs/io_vcf.md` reports gave
21109 of 23038, 91.6 in 100, for the same function. The two checks added
since, an allele below the missing one and the allele numbers against
ALT, did not move the picture.

### 3.2 The VCF read on 18 threads: two fifths idle, one eighth in the scheduler

The same file and binary, 18 threads, 22 s. 19 threads at 6759 samples
each, 128421 in all; the main thread's 6759 are all `__psynch_cvwait` and
are not work, so the workers are 121662:

| | samples | share of the workers |
|---|---|---|
| `fill_row_genotypes`, the parse | 51997 | 42.7 in 100 |
| `__psynch_cvwait`, asleep | 48245 | 39.7 in 100 |
| `__psynch_mutexwait` | 7633 | 6.3 in 100 |
| `swtch_pri`, yielding in a spin | 7407 | 6.1 in 100 |
| `memchr_aligned`, the search for the end of a line | 1391 | 1.1 in 100 |
| `read`, from the file system | 1026 | 0.8 in 100 |
| `__psynch_mutexdrop` | 524 | 0.4 in 100 |
| `from_utf8`, the nine first columns | 431 | 0.4 in 100 |
| `_platform_memmove`, the copy of a line | 372 | 0.3 in 100 |

Two fifths of the workers' time is sleep and **12.8 in 100 is rayon's own
mutex and yield machinery**, `__psynch_mutexwait` and `swtch_pri` and
`__psynch_mutexdrop` together. That is the cost of forking and joining the
pool once per batch with a serial read between the joins, and it is larger
than everything the reader does outside the parse. The read of the file
and the search for its line ends together are 2 in 100 of the workers.

This is why 18 cores give 6.0 times and not more. `docs/specs/io_vcf.md`
reports the same shape from the other side: the serial line pass was 4308
of the 9363 samples of the reading thread, and the workers idle in 61 in
100 of theirs.

### 3.3 What the serial line pass is made of

Nobody had split those 4308 samples before this review. They are in the
kept profile of 21 September, `profile_18threads.txt`, and they say that
the serial pass is not the `read` syscall:

```
+ 4308 popnei::io::vcf::VcfReader<BufReader<File>>::next_block  vcf.rs:1216
+ ! 1887 std::io::read_until<WithFirstBytes<BufReader<File>>>  mod.rs:2007
+ ! : 1887 core::slice::memchr::memchr_aligned
+ ! 1810 std::io::read_until<...>  mod.rs:2002
+ ! : 1810 <File as Read>::read_buf
+ ! :   1810 read  (in libsystem_kernel.dylib)
+ ! 554 std::io::read_until<...>  mod.rs:2009
+ ! : 550 _platform_memmove
+ ! 15 ... RawVecInner::reserve ... _realloc
+ 4063 ... next_block ... popnei::io::vcf::parse_rows
```

Of the 9363 samples of the reading thread: **the search for the end of a
line is 1887, 20 in 100**; the `read` from the file system is 1810, 19 in
100; the copy of each line out of the file's buffer into the batch text is
554, 6 in 100; the parse is 4063.

The search for the end of a line being the largest of the three is what
finding H1 rests on.

### 3.4 The vars file: lz4 in both directions, and almost nothing else

`/usr/bin/sample`, 25 s, on `big20000.vars`, 1000 diploid individuals and
20000 variants in four record batches, 16280410 bytes, held in memory,
one thread. The benchmark times three things in one process, a pass with
the genotypes alone, a pass with every field, and the write of the same
blocks into a vector of bytes, so one profile covers all three. 16168
samples of the thread that works:

| | samples | share |
|---|---|---|
| `lz4_flex` compressing, the write | 7168 | 44.3 in 100 |
| `lz4_flex` decompressing, the two reads | 6970 | 43.1 in 100 |
| `_platform_memmove` | 1103 | 6.8 in 100 |
| `popnei::io::vars::block_of_the_batch` | 242 | 1.5 in 100 |
| `__bzero`, the zeroing of each batch buffer | 135 | 0.8 in 100 |
| the benchmark's own sum of the genotypes | 119 | 0.7 in 100 |
| malloc and free together | about 120 | 0.7 in 100 |
| `alleles_column`, `chrom_column`, `id_column` of the write | 35 | 0.2 in 100 |

The write section is 7903 of the 16168 samples and `lz4_flex` is 7546 of
that section, so **the write is 95 in 100 lz4 compression**. This is the
first profile of the vars writer that has been taken; `docs/reports/vars-file.md`
inferred "nearly all of it the compression of `lz4_flex`" from the wall
time alone, and the inference was right.

The read is the same story from the other side, and it agrees with the
profile `docs/reports/vars-file.md` records: 88 in 100 of a pass inside
the decompression, 3 in 100 in the copy of the genotypes into the block,
3 in 100 in the benchmark's sum.

**So no change to popnei's own code can move the vars file much.** What is
left for popnei in the write is 357 samples, 4.5 in 100 of the write; in
the read, about 6 in 100. The vars file is as fast as lz4 is on these
bytes, and the three things that could change that are the compression
itself, compressing or decompressing batches side by side, and the
build settings under `lz4_flex`.

### 3.5 What the VCF reader is compared with

plink2 v2.0.0-a.7.7 reads `big.vcf` in 0.273 s on one thread; popnei takes
0.563 s; pyNei takes 13.5 s. The two faster numbers are not the same unit
of work, and `docs/reports/vcf-to-blocks.md` records what plink2's command
was, `--vcf <file> --make-pgen --threads 1`. Against popnei, plink2 does
**more**: it writes a pgen file, and `big_plink.log` in the benchmark
directory shows that run writing a temporary pgen and then the final one.
It also does **less** in a way no change to popnei can close: a pgen holds
2 bits a genotype where a popnei block holds a byte an allele, eight times
fewer bytes to store.

The spike, which the spec holds popnei to, does three things less than
popnei: it checks neither the ploidy of each genotype nor its allele
numbers against ALT, and it does not read the FILTER. The numbers review
found that none of the three can be dropped without a silently wrong
result: an allele of 5 at a two-allele site would be counted as a real
allele and reach a frequency, and a short genotype would read as half
missing. The FILTER costs 8 samples of 21139 and is already free.

Neither comparison is wrong, and they answer different questions. The
spike says whether popnei's parse is as fast as a Rust parse of the same
shape. plink2 says whether a user who wants their VCF in a fast file of
its own waits longer with popnei than with the tool they have. Section 4.6
says what would answer the second one.

Taken for this review at `d1c03f8`, the machine idle, the files in the
page cache, the bench profile. The VCF numbers are the best and the median
of 6 reads of `big.vcf` and `big.vcf.gz`; the vars numbers are the best of

## 4. The measurement plan

In the order in which it unblocks the findings. Every command is run on a
quiet machine, one at a time.

### 4.1 Split the hottest function before changing it

91.2 in 100 of the one-thread read is inside `fill_row_genotypes`, and
`[profile.bench] debug = "line-tables-only"` gives `sample` no inlined
frames, so that share is one opaque block. Every finding of section 6.1
proposes a change inside it and none of them can be told apart by the
profile that exists.

Set `[profile.bench] debug = 2`, and take the profile with `samply`,
which resolves the inline frames:

```
samply record --save-only -o tmp/vcf.json.gz -r 2000 -- \
  target/release/deps/read_vcf-<hash> /Users/jose/devel/popnei-bench/big.vcf \
  --threads 1 --runs 60
```

The gate: the 19289 samples split among `gt_of`, `fill_row_genotype`,
`parse_row_allele` and the tab search. Until they do, the order of the
findings inside that function is a guess.

The deterministic half of this was run for this review and needs no quiet
machine. `cargo asm --lib -p popnei --simplify
"popnei::io::vcf::fill_row_genotypes"` gives 887 lines, and in them:

- the function's stack frame is **416 bytes**, `sub sp, sp, #416`;
- **12 call sites of `alloc::fmt::format::format_inner` and
  `__rustc::__rust_alloc`** are inlined into it, which is the error
  messages built with `format!` sitting inside the hottest loop of popnei;
- `ByteColumns::next` is called **out of line twice**, so the tab search
  is not always inlined;
- the tab search that is inlined **is vectorized**, 64 bytes an iteration
  with four `cmeq.16b`, so `memchr` is doing what it was chosen for.

Those four numbers are the before side of the gates of findings H1, H2 and
L1.

### 4.2 The allocation count, which does not exist yet

Add to `crates/popnei/Cargo.toml` a dev-dependency `dhat = "0.3"` and a
feature `dhat-heap = []`, and in `read_vcf.rs` and `vars_file.rs` only:

```rust
#[cfg(feature = "dhat-heap")]
#[global_allocator]
static ALLOC: dhat::Alloc = dhat::Alloc;
```

with `let _profiler = dhat::Profiler::new_heap();` as the first line of
`main` behind the same `cfg`. A dev-dependency of the core crate is not
built when the binding crates depend on it, so it cannot reach the wheel,
the npm package or either wasm target.

```
cargo bench --features dhat-heap --bench read_vcf -- \
  /Users/jose/devel/popnei-bench/big.vcf --threads 1 --runs 1
cargo bench --features dhat-heap --bench vars_file -- \
  /Users/jose/devel/popnei-bench/big20000.vars --runs 1
```

Read `total blocks` and `blocks at t-gmax` from `dhat-heap.json`. dhat
takes a backtrace per allocation, so its wall time means nothing: the
count is the gate and the time is taken in a second run without the
feature. This is the baseline every allocation finding is gated on, and
taking it needs no quiet machine.

### 4.3 Two repairs the benchmarks need before they can decide anything

**The VCF benchmark never consumes the genotypes.** `read_vcf.rs` drops
each block and reads only `block.num_vars`, so the parse writes into a
vector that is freed unread. Dead store elimination across a free is
legal, and whole program inlining is what would expose it, so a run that
got faster under LTO could be measuring the parse being deleted. Add
`std::hint::black_box(&block.gts)`, which costs nothing and only forces
the pointer to escape. Do this before the LTO experiment, not after.

**The VCF benchmark can only ask for the genotypes.** `Needs::GTS` is
hardcoded, so the 38 ms of 650 that `docs/specs/io_vcf.md` attributes to
the other five columns cannot be reproduced from the repository, and
finding L2 cannot be gated. Add a flag that sets the fields.

Neither repair changes the library, and both are prerequisites, not
findings.

### 4.4 A target for the vars writer

The write is 39.0 to 39.3 ms for four blocks into memory and 95 in 100 of
it is lz4. Every other target in these two specs has an outside program on
its other side: the spike for the VCF, arrow-rs alone for the vars read.
What would give the write one is a second mode of `vars_file.rs` that
writes the same record batches with `arrow_ipc::writer::FileWriter` and
lz4 and nothing of popnei, in the same process and the same run. The gap
between the two is what popnei's writer costs; the arrow-rs number is the
target, as it is for the read.

The owner also decides the unit: the write into memory, 39 ms, or the
`write_vars` call as a Python user makes it on the same panel of 20000
variants, 66.5 to 73.7 ms, which holds
the VCF parse and a `sync_all` of 4.8 to 7.3 ms.

### 4.5 The first measurement of the VCF reader in wasm

The VCF reader has never been run in either wasm target. Follow the shape
`js/popnei/bench/time_kosman_dists.mjs` already has: a sibling script that
takes a path and a number of runs, reads the file into a typed array and
opens it outside the clock, runs one untimed pass, then times a pass over
the blocks with the genotypes alone asked for, and prints the best, the
median and the worst beside the bytes of the wasm memory before and after.
Run it over `big.vcf` and then over `big.vcf.gz`. For the first profile of
the VCF reader in wasm, `node --cpu-prof` on one run, built without the
step that strips the name section, which is what the Kosman review had to
do to get names.

What to expect, and it is an estimate from native numbers and not a
measurement: **0.7 to 1.1 s** for the 403 MB file on one thread of a
worker, against 0.563 s natively on one thread. The range is the three
wasm-to-native ratios this repository has: 1.18 for the spike's own VCF
parser under pyodide, 1.53 for the vars reader before `+simd128`, and 1.94
for the Kosman pairs phase. The genotype parse is a branchy per byte loop,
so it is closest in shape to the pairs phase, and `+simd128` will not
vectorize it, which is why the flag should buy the VCF read much less than
the 30 in 100 it bought the vars read.

### 4.6 popnei against plink2, in the unit a user sees

`hyperfine` times a command and reports the spread over runs:

```
hyperfine -w 1 -r 10 \
  'plink2 --vcf /Users/jose/devel/popnei-bench/big.vcf --make-pgen --threads 1 --out tmp/p' \
  'uv run python -c "import popnei; popnei.write_vars(popnei.open_vcf(\"/Users/jose/devel/popnei-bench/big.vcf\"), \"tmp/p.vars\")"'
```

Both go from a VCF to their own file on one thread. That is the comparison
goal 4 of the objectives asks for, and it is the one a user feels. Run it
after `uv run maturin develop --release`.

## 5. The build configuration

`[profile.release]` sets `overflow-checks = false` and nothing else:
optimisation level 3, no link time optimisation, 16 codegen units, no
`target-cpu`. `[profile.bench]` inherits it and adds line tables.

- **Link time optimisation and one codegen unit have never been built.**
  `docs/reports/vars-file.md` named this and it was never run: for a pass
  that is 88 in 100 inside another crate, it is the one build setting
  worth trying. The VCF reader calls `memchr` across a crate boundary and
  the profile shows `memchr::arch::aarch64::neon::memchr::One::find_raw`
  as a frame of its own, so at least some of those calls are not inlined.
  Three builds, one change each: `codegen-units = 1`, then `lto = "thin"`,
  then `lto = "fat"`, each measured alone on both benchmarks, kept above
  2 in 100 in two clean measurements a side. **Run the `black_box` repair
  of section 4.3 first**, or an inlined-away parse will look like a gain.
- **The numbers review cleared these three of any effect on a result.**
  All the arithmetic of both modules is integer but for the quality, which
  is `str::parse::<f32>`, correctly rounded and not reassociable, and Rust
  does not reassociate floats without an explicit algebraic operation.
  So link time optimisation, one codegen unit and `target-cpu` can be run
  on these two modules without a numbers review each time. The one
  exception: a binary built with `target-cpu=native` is not the binary a
  wheel ships, so a number taken with it is not popnei's number.
- **`+simd128` is already set for both wasm targets**, in
  `.cargo/config.toml`, since commit `7f3b6cc`. What it is worth for a VCF
  parse or an lz4 decompression, as opposed to the bit counting it was
  turned on for, has not been measured.
- **The pyodide wheel may be losing that flag**, which is finding H6.
- **An allocator swap is not a candidate** at the allocation rates these
  modules run at, and `wasm-opt -O3` cannot be tried: it comes from binaryen,
  the toolkit that rewrites a compiled WebAssembly module to a smaller and
  faster one, and binaryen is not installed on this machine.

## 6. The findings

**The order to run them in.** Each one earns its place by the share of a
profile it sits in and by how little it costs to try:

1. **H6**, the pyodide wheel losing the vector instructions. It is a grep
   over a build log, it needs no quiet machine, and it is worth more to a
   notebook in a tab than anything else here: the same flag took the wasm
   vars read from 0.162 s to 0.113 s.
2. **H1**, the end of a line searched with the scalar routine, 20 in 100
   of the reading thread. About 20 lines, copying a loop that is already
   in the same module, and it helps the wasm build too. Two categories
   found it on their own.
3. **H4**, the common diploid genotype in one pass, inside the function
   that is 91 in 100 of the one-thread read.
4. **H5**, the error messages moved out of that function's body. Its
   before side is already measured and it is four small functions.
5. **H3**, the read of one batch overlapped with the parse of the one
   before, 12.8 in 100 of worker time in the scheduler. Instrument first:
   the instrumentation alone decides whether to build anything.
6. **H2**, every byte of the file copied twice. Build the cheap half only.
7. The build settings of section 5, after the benchmark repair of 4.3.
8. **L5**, **L6**, the two passes over each batch of the vars file, which
   is where the 0.7 ms of margin against its target would come from.
9. Everything else, which needs a measurement that does not exist yet.

### 6.1 The VCF reader, the serial line pass

**H1. The end of a line is searched with the scalar routine while the
vector one is already linked in.** `vcf.rs:451-457`. Hot path, high
confidence, and **two categories found it independently**, input and
output and the wasm build. **Applied, in `76b0bfd`; section 9 has the two
numbers.**

`read_line` calls `BufRead::read_until(b'\n', line)` for a plain and for a
gzipped source, and `read_until` searches with `core::slice::memchr`, the
standard library's scalar scan a machine word at a time, over every one of
the 403572954 bytes of the file. That search is **1887 of the 9363 samples
of the reading thread on 18 threads, 20 in 100**, and 717 of 21139 on one.
The `memchr` crate is already a dependency and its vector routine for this
machine is already in the binary: the profile shows
`memchr::arch::aarch64::neon::memchr::One::find_raw` as a frame of its
own, reached from `filter_passed`, with 9 samples.

The shape to copy is in the same module. `BgzfReader::read_line` at
`bgzf.rs:199-235` already does `fill_buf`, then `memchr::memchr`, then
`extend_from_slice`, then `consume`, which is why the bgzipped arm already
gets the vector search and the other two do not.

In wasm the same change matters more, not less: there a machine word is
4 bytes, so the standard library's scan does 4 bytes an iteration, and the
`memchr` crate dispatches to its 128 bit routine under exactly the
`target_feature` that both wasm builds already set.
Gate: the wall time at 18 threads, where the serial pass is what bounds
the reader, kept if the best of 5 falls below 0.093 s by more than the
0.004 s the batch sweeps moved; then a new profile, in which the 1887 has
to shrink. The one-thread read must not get worse than 0.563 s. The
one-thread benchmark cannot see this change on its own: its spread is
0.558 to 0.630 s.
Cost: about 20 lines, one free function over `BufRead` shared by `vcf.rs`
and `bgzf.rs`, one more use of a dependency that is already there, no
unsafe and no lifetime. The contract to keep is the one `read_until` has:
the end of line stays in the buffer and a source at its end gives 0. The
tests that feed the reader one byte at a time cover it.

**H2. Every byte of the file is copied twice before it is parsed.**
`vcf.rs:909`. Hot path, high confidence for the cost and medium for the
remedy. The kernel copies into the 256 KiB buffer of the file, 1540 `read`
calls for this file, and `read_until` then copies each line out of that
buffer into the batch text. Those are the 1810 `read` samples and the 554
memmove samples of section 3.3, 25 in 100 of the reading thread together.

The copy-free version, cutting the lines over the source's own buffer with
the rows pointing into it, **cannot work as the code stands**: a batch is
4096 lines or 16 MiB and spans many fills of a 256 KiB buffer, and the
batch text has to outlive the parse. It would need the buffer to be as
large as a batch, a carry of the tail line across fills, and the bound of
a batch to become "what the buffer holds", which the settled sweep of
4096 lines, 16 MiB and 256 KiB did not cover.
So build only the cheap half: fill the batch text in buffer-sized pieces
and find the newlines in it with `memchr_iter`. That leaves one copy and
about 25 `read` calls a pass.
Gate: counts first, which are exact — `read` calls a pass, 1540 now, and
bytes moved, 403572954 now. Note what the buffer sweep already says: from
8 KiB to 1 MiB the read went 0.105, 0.095, 0.093, 0.093 s, so the syscall
count is not what the 1810 samples are. They are the kernel's copy, and
only the 554 goes away.

**H3. The read of a batch and the parse of the batch before it never
overlap.** `vcf.rs:1030-1083`. Hot path, high confidence. `build_block` is
a strict alternation: read 4096 lines serially, parse them on 18 threads,
append serially, repeat, 40 times for this file. While the reader runs the
workers sleep; while they parse the source is untouched. The 18-thread
profile measures what that costs: 39.7 in 100 of worker time asleep and
**12.8 in 100 in rayon's mutex and yield machinery**, which is the pool
being put to sleep and woken 40 times.

The read ahead thread of section 3 of `docs/architecture.md` is the named,
planned, unbuilt answer, and this finding is not a discovery of it. What
is new is the number: the serial part accounts for about 0.043 s of the
0.093 s, so perfect overlap puts the floor near 0.050 s and no more than
0.043 s is on the table.

The error guarantee survives, and cheaply: `fill_batch` already parks its
error in a field that `build_block` consumes at the top of the *next*
iteration, so an error of the reader is already one batch behind the
blocks it must follow, which is exactly what "the blocks before a wrong
line are given, then the error, in file order" needs.

The cheaper first step, and the one to try first: `rayon::join` of the
read of the next batch against the parse of the batch in hand. No extra
thread, so nothing that wasm lacks, and no channel, so the error path
stays a field. The price in both shapes is the borrow: `fill_batch` takes
all of `self`, so the two halves need disjoint borrows, which means
lifting the line reading into a type of its own or a free function.
Gate, in two steps. First **instrument, do not guess**: three durations
accumulated around the read, the parse and the append, printed from the
benchmark, at 1 and at 18 threads. A sampling profile cannot see a
sleeping worker, and the performance review skill's note on what this machine can and
cannot measure prescribes exactly this.
At 18 threads the read should come out at 0.040 to 0.046 s; under 0.020 s
refutes the finding and the read ahead is not worth building. Only then
build the `join` version, kept if the 18-thread median falls below 0.075 s
with the blocks byte-identical at 1 and 18 threads.

**Run H1 before H3.** H1 takes work out of the serial pass and H3 hides
what is left of it behind the parse; if H1 lands first, H3 has less to
hide and its instrumentation says so honestly.

**L10. The bgzipped read is bounded by inflating on one thread, and
flate2 has another pure-Rust backend.** `bgzf.rs:514-533` and the
dependency line. Likely, medium confidence, **and no profile of a
bgzipped read has ever been taken**. From the wall times alone: plain
0.563 s against bgzipped 0.890 s on one thread, and 0.093 s against
0.394 s on 18. The extra is 0.327 s and 0.301 s, the same work at both
thread counts, so it is serial on the reading thread.
popnei takes `flate2` on its default backend, `miniz_oxide`. flate2 also
has a `zlib-rs` backend, pure Rust with no C and no build script, and
`zlib-rs` is already resolved in `Cargo.lock`. popnei's own code would not
change: the three calls it makes and the CRC type all exist under that
backend.
Gate, in two steps, and the first is 30 seconds of profiler: profile a
bgzipped read and read what share of the reading thread is inside the
inflater. That decides whether to spend anything here or on the parallel
members the module's own header already describes. Only then swap the
backend and measure against 0.890 s and 0.394 s, keeping it **only if it
also passes `cargo wasm-check`, the alias that builds the crate for both
wasm targets and denies a warning,** and the wasm read does
not get slower under node.

**L4. The chromosome name hashed once per variant.** `vcf.rs:1001`.
Likely, medium confidence. `chroms.intern(&row.chrom)` hashes a `String`
key with the default hasher for every variant, on the serial thread that
bounds the 18-thread read. The vars reader already does the cheaper thing:
`chrom_numbers` keeps the last name and its number and interns only when
the name changes, which for a sorted VCF is once per chromosome.
Gate: a count of the calls that reach the map, 100000 today and about 1
with the cache, which is the same on every run; then the 18-thread wall
time, kept above 1 ms of 93.

### 6.2 The VCF reader, inside the hot function

**H4. The common diploid genotype taken in one pass.** `vcf.rs:1776-1800`.
Hot path, high confidence. For a genotype written `0/1` followed by a tab,
the code makes four passes over three bytes and two `memchr` searches: the
tab that ends the column, the colon of `gt_of` that is never found when
the FORMAT is `GT` alone, the test for a leading separator, and the split
on `/` or `|`; then per allele a comparison with `.`, an emptiness test,
`char::from(byte).to_digit(10)`, a saturating multiply and add per digit,
an `i8::try_from` and a comparison with the number of alleles. One
four-byte read and six comparisons decide all of it and write both
alleles. The general path stays for everything else, entered at the same
cursor, so every refusal of the spec survives: an allele above what ALT
declares, a genotype of another ploidy, `-1` and `-0` written as alleles,
`/0/1`, `0/1:99` under a longer FORMAT, and a missing genotype each take
today's code and give today's message with the line and the individual
named. In wasm the gain is larger in relative terms, because there the
four passes are paid with a scalar byte search.
Gate: the instruction count of the per-genotype block in `cargo asm`, then
`cargo bench --bench read_vcf -- big.vcf --threads 1 --runs 5`, three
interleaved sets a side, kept above 0.032 s of the 0.563 s. Then
`--threads 18` must not rise. Then the same on a VCF whose columns carry
the four other values a called VCF has, which must not rise by more than
0.005 s.
Cost: about 25 lines beside the general loop, and one invariant written at
the arm: accept only what the general path accepts, fall back for
everything else. No unsafe, no indexing, no new bare operator. The owner
took this shape before, in `67b7a16` for the Kosman distances.

**H5. The error messages built inside the hottest loop.** `vcf.rs:1884`,
with `:1780` and `:1790`. Hot path, medium confidence, and the before side
is already measured: the function's frame is 416 bytes and 12 call sites
of `format_inner` and `__rust_alloc` are inlined into it. `Error` is 72
bytes, so `parse_row_allele` returns a `Result<i8, Error>` of 80 bytes
through a return slot, once per allele, 2e8 times. Marking the message
builders `#[cold]` and `#[inline(never)]` moves that code out of the loop
body and shrinks the frame. Every message is the same string.
Gate: the frame size and the count of inlined format sites in `cargo asm`,
which are exact and need no quiet machine, then the same `read_vcf`
command kept above 2 in 100.
Cost: four small cold functions. The owner took this shape before, in
`7d29ae4` and `e5b0f25`.
The reviewers disagreed on one point worth recording: the allocations
review checked every `format!` in these functions and found each one
inside the branch that returns the error, so the allocation is not paid on
the fall-through path. What H5 removes is therefore the code size and the
frame, not an allocation per genotype. That is a smaller claim than it
first looks, and the assembly is what decides it.

**L1. The eight tabs of the nine first columns found twice per line.**
`vcf.rs:1667` and `:1684`. Likely, medium confidence.
`memchr_iter(b'\t', line).nth(8)` walks the first eight tabs and throws
their positions away, and `head.split('\t')` then finds the same eight
again. Keeping the nine positions gives each column with no second search.
The profile puts the per-line work at 970 of 21139 samples, 4.6 in 100:
`memchr_aligned` 717, `from_utf8` 235, `Split<char>::next` 98,
`next_column` 18, `BatchRow::parse` 31.
Gate: the `memchr_aligned` line of a new profile against 717, then the
wall time kept above 0.010 s.

**L2. The per-digit saturating arithmetic.** `vcf.rs:1907`. Likely,
medium confidence. `saturating_mul(10).saturating_add(digit)` is a
multiply, a compare and a select per digit where the loop itself can
establish the bound: if the loop ends the allele at the first digit that
takes the number above 127, then at the top of every iteration the number
is at most 127, `number * 10 + digit` cannot overflow a `u32`, and the
`i8::try_from` cannot fail. One error message changes, `128x` would say
"is above 127" instead of "is not an allele number", and no test covers
that case today, so a test for it is part of the change. The guard against
the silent version of this is already in the tree: `[profile.test]` keeps
overflow checks on and there are tests that read `0/4294967296` and
`0/99999999999999999999`, so a wrong bound panics in `cargo test` instead
of wrapping in release.
Cost: one `#[expect(clippy::arithmetic_side_effects)]` carrying the bound,
the shape `variant.rs:366` already uses, and one new test.

**L3. The individuals walked for a name only the errors use.**
`vcf.rs:1778`. Likely, medium confidence. `individuals.iter().enumerate()`
yields a `&String` that is coerced to `&str` for the error arms; if the
compiler does not sink that load into the cold branches, each worker reads
16 bytes per individual per variant for a value no successful parse uses.
Gate: count the loads from the individuals' base register inside the loop
in `cargo asm`. Zero loads closes it with no run at all.

### 6.3 The vars file

**L5. Each batch buffer zeroed before it is read over.** `vars.rs:2771`.
Likely, medium confidence. `bytes_at` does `try_reserve_exact` and then
`resize(len, 0)`, which writes the whole batch as zeroes that `read_exact`
immediately overwrites: 16.28 MB of zeroing per pass over this panel. The
profile puts `__bzero` at 135 samples, 0.8 in 100 of the process, and the
pass it is in has 0.7 ms of margin against its 21 ms target.
`read_to_end` over a `take` fills the spare capacity without zeroing,
because `BufReader<File>` and `Cursor` both specialise `read_buf`; a wasm
source that does not specialise keeps today's behaviour. The `Vec` itself
has to stay: `Buffer::from(bytes)` takes it with no copy.
Gate: the wall time of the genotypes-alone pass, best of 20, three
interleaved sets, kept above 0.15 ms of 20.17.

**L6. The genotypes of a batch walked twice.** `vars.rs:2363` and `:2375`.
Likely, medium confidence. `first_allele_below_the_missing_one` reads the
whole 10 MB decompressed buffer, and `extend_from_slice` then reads it
again and writes 10 MB. In chunks of 64 KiB, copy a chunk then check it,
each chunk is checked while it is still in the nearest cache and the pass
reads the buffer once. The check itself is already as cheap as it can be:
the numbers review confirmed the fold over `i8::min` is exact, empty-safe,
vectorisable and reads every allele once, at 74 to 114 GB/s, and that no
cheaper correct predicate exists because the valid set is 0 to 127 plus
255 as bytes, which is not one unsigned range.
The risk is the error's variant number, which must have the chunk offset
added to it, and the existing tests of that error must cover an allele in
a later chunk.
Gate: best of 20 against 20.17 ms, kept above 0.4 ms.

**L7. The write compresses on one thread between two parallel reads.**
`vars.rs:758-767`. Likely, medium confidence. `write_vars` reads a block
on 18 cores, then compresses it on one for about 9.8 ms, then reads the
next; each stage is idle while the other runs. lz4 inside one batch is one
10 MB buffer, so across blocks is the only parallelism there is. This is
the mirror of the read ahead and is **not** in section 3 of the
architecture, which names read ahead only.
Cost, honestly: `write_block` must be cut in two, because building the
arrays needs the chromosome table, a borrow the writer thread cannot hold.
The reading side builds the record batch and sends it over a channel of
capacity one; the writer thread compresses and pushes its entry in arrival
order, which is file order. The sink must become `Send`, a write error has
to come back across the channel, and the whole thing is behind
`cfg(not(target_family = "wasm"))` with today's loop as the wasm twin.
Gate: the bytes must be identical to the serial writer's, and the Python
end-to-end median must fall below 60 ms from 66.5.
**This is the one finding whose experiment should wait**: it is the
largest change in the review, and it would be judged against a target that
does not exist yet (section 4.4).

**N1. Reading a batch ahead buys nothing in the benchmark.** The vars read
is 88 in 100 lz4 and the batches are independent, so reading one batch
ahead in a thread of its own is the obvious move. It buys nothing that
`vars_file.rs` could see, because that benchmark's consumer only sums the
genotypes, 3 in 100 of the pass: the pipeline would still be decompression
bound. It buys up to a batch's decompression in a pass whose consumer
works as long as the decompression does, which is what the vars read is
for. `docs/specs/io_vars.md` already defers it to the read ahead of
`docs/specs/block.md`, and the recommendation is to leave it there and
measure it with the first calculation that consumes blocks.

### 6.4 The Python boundary

The boundary is closed and the review found nothing to change on the
default path. Counted from the code: `write_vars` is **one** crossing, the
whole loop inside one `py.detach`; `iter_blocks` over a million variants
of 1000 individuals is **202** crossings, one per block plus two. There is
no per-variant or per-individual crossing anywhere in `python/popnei/`.
The genotypes, the positions and the qualities reach numpy with **no
copy**, through `read_only(x.into_pyarray(py))`, and the genotypes-only
path builds five `None`s and one array object per block.

**L8. The id and alleles columns build three Python objects per variant.**
`popnei-python/src/source.rs:604` and `:612`. Likely, medium confidence,
pattern only. For a block of 5000 variants the id column allocates 5001
objects and the alleles column 5000 tuples and 10000 strings at ploidy
two, each an allocation, a refcount and a second UTF-8 validation. The
chromosome column does not have this shape: it makes one string per
distinct chromosome and shares it. Worth acting on only if the fields add
more than 15 in 100 to a pass, and the cheap fixes are already taken, so
the real option is a lazily built column, which is a large change.
Gate: a profile of the Python process, `sample <pid>`, with `fields=()`
against every field, which is also the first profile of popnei as Python
calls it.

**N2. `sync_all` is 7 to 11 in 100 of a `write_vars` call, and it is
right to keep.** `popnei-python/src/vars.rs:172`. It is the only place the
file system's verdict on the bytes can be read: a `close` cannot report in
Rust, and the owner's rule is that a wrong or half written result never
passes in silence. Dropping it would not protect a user whose process
dies, because the page cache survives that and the kernel writes the file
anyway; it would only stop protecting against a power cut, and it would
turn a file system that reports "no space left" at allocation time into
silence. On macOS `sync_all` is the full flush of the drive's cache and
`sync_data` is the same call, so there is no cheaper correct variant.
Whether it should become an argument a caller can turn off is the owner's,
and it is a change of a promise, not an experiment.

### 6.5 The allocations

**L9. The id column is one allocation per variant where every other text
column is one buffer.** `vcf.rs:1007` and `vars.rs:2179`. Likely, medium
confidence. `Block.id` is a `Vec<String>`, so each variant costs a malloc,
a free and 24 bytes of header. `AllelesColumn` in `block.rs` already
refuses exactly this, and its doc comment says why: "a block of 10000
variants is 10000 allocations either way, and one buffer makes it one".
`docs/specs/io_vcf.md` names the ids as "the one column that allocates for
each variant" inside the 38 ms of 650 that every column costs on one
thread.
Gate: split the 38 ms first, with every column against every column but
the id, best of 20. If the id is worth under 10 ms of the 38, drop this
finding. This is the largest change in the report after the write-behind:
a new column type and six call sites across three crates.

**N3. The block's genotypes are filled with the missing allele and then
overwritten.** `vcf.rs:1052`. `_platform_memset` is 61 of 21139 samples,
0.3 in 100, and 2e8 bytes a pass for this file. It cannot be removed
without writing into uninitialised memory, which `#![forbid(unsafe_code)]`
refuses. It can only go when blocks are recycled, which is the read ahead
work. Not actionable on its own; handed to whoever builds H3 as a number
to check.

### 6.6 The wasm builds

**H6. The pyodide wheel is built without the vector instructions.**
`scripts/build_pyodide_wheel.sh` and `.cargo/config.toml`. Hot path for
the wheel, high confidence on the mechanism, and one step of the chain was
read from pyodide's source rather than watched in a build.

`+simd128` is set for both wasm targets in `.cargo/config.toml`, and it is
worth a great deal: the Kosman review measured the wasm vars read falling
from 0.162 s to 0.113 s when the flag went on, because it vectorizes the
lz4 decompression that is 88 in 100 of that pass.

Three things were checked for this review and each one holds.
`scripts/build_pyodide_wheel.sh` sets no `RUSTFLAGS` of any kind; it calls
`pyodide build` and nothing else. In the pyodide build environment on this
machine, `pyodide_build/config.py` sets its default `rustflags` to
`-C link-arg=-sSIDE_MODULE=2 -C link-arg=-sWASM_BIGINT`, with no
`+simd128` in it, and exports that key as
`CARGO_TARGET_WASM32_UNKNOWN_EMSCRIPTEN_RUSTFLAGS`. And cargo takes such
an environment variable **in place of** a target's rustflags in a config
file rather than joining the two, which is the rule
`.cargo/config.toml` already writes out in its own comment for a different
reason. So the wheel is built without the flag and popnei's own code
behind `cfg(target_feature = "simd128")` is not compiled into it.

What looked like evidence against this resolves the other way. The earlier
review counted the vector opcode "in the package's wasm 12 times and in
the wheel's library 9", and a count of the same byte pattern over the
wheel that is in `dist/` today gives 6 against the npm package's 12. But
SIMD in the wheel does not have to come from the flag: the workspace
manifest says that gemm's kernels "carry their own annotation", so they
vectorize whatever the global flag is. A byte count over a whole module
cannot tell those apart, and it is not the gate.

Gate: a verbose pyodide build with the rustc lines grepped for the flag,
against the same grep on the kept npm build log. A count of 0 on the rustc
lines settles it. No wall time needed. What is left to confirm is only
that pyodide-build exports the variable during a real `pyodide build` on
this machine, which is the one step of the chain that was reasoned from
its source rather than watched.
The fix, if it is real, is not to copy pyodide's link arguments into
popnei's script, which goes stale the moment pyodide changes them. It is
to export whether the flag was on from both binding crates and assert it
in the pyodide smoke test and in a node test, so that no build can lose it
in silence again.

**L11. A batch of one line in wasm has never been measured.**
`vcf.rs:130`. Likely, medium confidence, pattern only. In wasm a batch is
one line, so the work done once per batch natively — building the rules,
resizing the block's genotypes, appending the columns — runs once per
variant. The sweep that settled the native constant covered 256 to 4096
lines on 18 threads and says nothing about 1. The bytes filled with the
missing allele are the same either way, so what is at stake is the fixed
cost per batch and it may well be nothing, which is the reason to count it
rather than argue it. The choice is a win for the tab in memory: the text
buffer holds about 4 KB instead of 16 MiB.
Gate: build the package with the constant at 1, 64 and 1024 and run the
node script of section 4.5, reading the wasm memory as the existing memory
test does. Keep a larger batch only if it takes 5 in 100 or more off the
read **and** grows the memory by less than 16 MiB.

**N4. A tab cannot stream a VCF larger than its memory, and section 11 of
the architecture says it can.** `crates/popnei-js/src/source.rs`. Not a
speed finding, and it bounds every wasm number above.
The only source the JavaScript binding has is bytes already in memory:
`open_vcf` and `open_vars` each take a vector that wasm-bindgen fills from
a typed array, one copy, shared by every pass. There is no source backed
by the call that reads a range of a picked file inside a web worker, which
section 11 of `docs/architecture.md` describes. So the whole file sits in
the module's memory for the life of the source, and wasm never gives that
memory back.
The arithmetic, not a measurement: reading `big.vars` in a tab peaks near
107 MB and turning `big.vcf` into a vars file peaks near 510 MB, and both
fit. The objectives' largest dataset does not, by an order of magnitude: a
million variants of 10000 individuals is about 40 GB of VCF and a 7.8 GB
vars file, both past the 4 GB a wasm module can address. At a tab that
gives wasm 2 GB the ceiling is about 450000 variants of 1000 individuals,
or 45000 of 10000 — and that is the file, not the calculation.
The failure is clean, which was checked rather than assumed: the reserve
of the whole length, the block size check, the batch reader and the writer
all go through the fallible path and give an error, and no path to an
abort was found. This is for the owner and for whoever plans the
TypeScript work, not an experiment.

## 9. The experiments

One has been run. The rest of section 6 is a plan and nothing else of the
two modules was changed by this review.

### H1, the vector search for the end of a line: applied, `76b0bfd`

`read_line_of`, a free function over any `BufRead` that does `fill_buf`,
then `memchr::memchr`, then `extend_from_slice`, then `consume`, and
retries an interrupted read as `read_until` does. It is the loop
`BgzfReader::read_line` already had, lifted so that the plain and the
gzipped sources use it too; the bgzipped arm is untouched. 99 lines added
and 3 removed in one file, of which 43 are a test.

On `big.vcf`, 100000 variants of 1000 diploid individuals, 403572954
bytes, on the owner's Apple M5 Pro of 18 cores, the machine idle, one
command at a time, best of 6:

| | before, `ed1b506` | after, `76b0bfd` |
|---|---|---|
| plain, 18 threads | 0.087 s | 0.079 s |
| plain, 1 thread | 0.570 s | 0.571 s |

**The 18-thread read falls by 0.008 s, 9 in 100, and the one-thread read
does not move.** That is what the profile predicted and it is the shape to
expect: on one thread the whole serial line pass is 6 in 100 of the read
and the newline search is 3.4 of that, so taking a fifth off it is lost in
the spread; on 18 threads the serial pass is what bounds the reader, 4308
of the 9363 samples of the reading thread, and the search is 1887 of them,
so the same saving shows up whole.

The subagent that ran the experiment reported the one-thread read falling
from 0.620 s to 0.584 s. Its baseline was taken while the machine was
still drifting, and 0.620 s is not this reader's one-thread number: the
quiet-machine baseline of section 3.6 is 0.570 s and the measurement above
was taken again afterwards. The one-thread claim does not hold; the
18-thread one does, and it was confirmed by interleaving the two binaries,
baseline, new, baseline, new, which gave 0.088, 0.081, 0.089, 0.078 s.

The result is unchanged, which was checked past the test suites: a sha256
over the genotypes, the chromosomes, the positions, the ids, the alleles
